A handmade pricing formula that allows for marketplace fees

Most pricing advice for makers is a multiplier: materials × 2, × 3, add a bit for your time. Multipliers are quick, but they hide the two things that decide whether a sale pays you: what your hours are worth, and the share the marketplace takes from the price. This is the longer version, worked through for one bar of soap.

The formula

  1. Materials per item. For each supply: what you paid ÷ how much you bought × how much one batch uses. Add the lines up and divide by the number of items in the batch.
  2. Your time per item. Hands-on minutes for the batch ÷ items × your hourly rate. Pick the rate you would have to pay someone else to do it, not zero.
  3. Overhead per item. A share of the things that wear out or get used up without being in the product: moulds, scales, electricity, packaging you don't count elsewhere.
  4. Solve for the price, so the fees and your profit both come out of it:

price = (cost per item + fixed fees per order) ÷ (1 − fee percentages − target margin)

Cost per item is steps 1 to 3 added together. The fee percentages and fixed fees depend on where you sell. Target margin is the share of the price you want left as profit after costs, your wage and fees.

Why you divide instead of adding a markup

Marketplace fees are a percentage of what the buyer pays. So if you add 30% to your cost, the fee is taken from that bigger number, and your 30% shrinks. Markup and margin are different things: a 30% markup on £5.00 gives £6.50, but £1.50 is only 23% of £6.50, and that's before any fees. Dividing by (1 − fees − margin) is the only way to land on the margin you asked for.

Worked example: a bar of soap on Etsy UK

Example figures for a batch of 10 bars. Use your own.

Oils and butters£9.40
Lye£0.60
Fragrance£3.00
Wrap and label, 40p × 10£4.00
Materials for the batch£17.00
Materials per bar (÷ 10)£1.70
Your time: 2 hours at £15/hour, ÷ 10 (12 minutes a bar)£3.00
Overhead share per bar£0.30
Cost per bar£5.00

Now the fees. A UK Etsy seller pays 6.5% transaction, 4% + £0.20 processing and a 0.48% Regulatory Operating fee, and without a VAT ID Etsy adds 20% VAT on those fees. (All rates, with Etsy's source pages, are in our Etsy fees UK breakdown.) With postage built into the price and no VAT ID:

fee percentages = (6.5% + 4% + 0.48%) × 1.2 = 13.18%
fixed fee = £0.20 × 1.2 = £0.24

For a 30% margin:

(£5.00 + £0.24) ÷ (1 − 0.1318 − 0.30) = £5.24 ÷ 0.5682 = £9.22

Round up to £9.50. At £9.50 the fees are £1.49 and you keep £3.01 a bar, about 32%, on top of your £15 an hour. The listing fee is left out here; it's charged when you list or renew. It is 0.20 USD, about £0.15 at the European Central Bank reference rate for 10 November 2026 (about £0.18 if VAT is added). Rounding up from £9.22 to £9.50 adds about £0.24 to your profit, so it covers that.

If a sale comes from one of Etsy's Offsite Ads, Etsy adds a 15% fee on the order total (How Etsy's Offsite Ads Work). At £9.50 that is £1.43 before any VAT, so the same bar earns you about £1.58 instead of £3.01.

Check the answer against the market

The formula tells you the lowest price that pays you properly. It doesn't tell you what buyers will pay. If similar items sell well above your number, you have room. If your number is well above what sells, look at the inputs before you cut the price: a cheaper supplier, a bigger batch, less time per item, or a smaller margin on the item that brings people in.
